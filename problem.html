The problem

Kenya imports every tonne of its road bitumen, and many roads fail early.

Three facts, each checked against a public source. Click any Evidence tag to see the source.

1. The import bill

Kenya buys all its road bitumen abroad.

Bitumen is the black glue that holds a road's stones together. It is a petroleum product, and Kenya has no refinery making it. Customs data from the UN shows what Kenya imported:

YearImportedValueAverage price
202350,592 tonnesUSD 27.5 millionabout USD 544 a tonne
202439,850 tonnesUSD 20.1 millionabout USD 506 a tonne

Evidence P1Evidence P2 At the exchange rates of those years, that is about KES 2.6 to 3.9 billion a year.

Where it comes from

Most of it comes from a handful of Gulf-region suppliers, mainly Iran and the UAE, with Kuwait and Oman as well. Their share rose from 72% of the weight in 2023 to 91% in 2024.

That is a concentration risk: much of the supply depends on one region and one shipping route. It is also a reason to need less imported bitumen.

Technical detail

Data: UN Comtrade public API, reporter Kenya (code 404), commodity HS 271320 (petroleum bitumen), flow imports. 2023: 50,591,822 kg, USD 27,511,093. 2024: 39,850,049 kg, USD 20,145,616. By weight in 2023: Iran 32.6%, Turkey 26.2%, UAE 21.2%, Kuwait 12.0%, Oman 2.2%, Bahrain 2.1%, Iraq 2.0%, India 1.0%. In 2024: UAE 36.6%, Iran 31.5%, Kuwait 12.2%, Oman 9.1%, China 4.3%, Turkey 3.8%, Bahrain 1.3%, India 1.3%.

Iraq provides only about 2% of the weight. Evidence P3

2. Roads that fail

Roads wear out early, and repairs are expensive.

The 2024/25 roads budget was KES 171.9 billion, of which KES 69.5 billion was for repairs and maintenance. Evidence P8

Kenya's heat adds to the strain. A study for the Great Lakes region puts the maximum pavement design temperature in Kenya at 64°C. Evidence P12

Why we mention the caveats. Waste that already has another use (like bagasse for mill power, or cooking oil for biodiesel) is not "free". Our design uses only a small share of any of these, and we say so rather than claim these wastes are unused.

So the question is:

Could part of that waste make a road binder that needs less imported bitumen and lasts longer?
