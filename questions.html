Hard questions

The tough questions, answered straight.

These are the questions judges, engineers and sceptics ask most. Each has a short answer and a detailed one, with sources. Where we don’t know yet, we say so, and say how we will find out.

Will the plastic harm the environment?

The plastic is melted into the road’s glue and locked in, and it replaces part of an imported fossil product. The main open questions are fumes during mixing, chemicals washing out, and particles when the road wears. We will measure all three in the pilot.

The detailed answer
  • Where the plastic goes. We use the “wet process”: shredded plastic is melted and blended into hot bitumen, so it becomes part of the binder rather than loose flakes. We use only polyethylene and polypropylene (bags, wrappers, caps). We exclude PVC, which can release chlorine compounds when heated.
  • Fumes during mixing. The evidence is mixed. One peer-reviewed study found that recycled plastics lowered overall fume emissions from bitumen at 160–200°C; another found that PVC and high doses of HDPE raised them. A 2026 review notes that field data are still lacking. Source E1
  • Chemicals washing out. Some studies found no harmful leaching; one 2025 study found certain trace elements above US limits for some waste-plastic mixes. There is no standard leaching test for asphalt yet. Source E3
  • The bigger picture. Today much of this plastic is burned in the open at Dandora, which is far worse for air and health. Roads that last longer also need fewer rebuilds, saving fuel and emissions.
  • What we will do. Keep mixing temperatures as low as possible (warm-mix), monitor fumes at the plant, and sample rainwater runoff from the pilot alongside a normal asphalt section.

What about microplastics and nanoplastics?

Honest answer: this is not fully known for any plastic road in the world. Early studies suggest far fewer particles than from tyre wear, but nanoplastics have barely been measured. We will not claim it is safe until we have measured it ourselves.

The detailed answer
  • Microplastics (under 5 mm). One study estimated that particle release from plastic-modified pavement is roughly a thousand times lower than the tyre-wear particles every road already produces. A 2022 abrasion test found that plastic used inside the binder can release particles earlier than plastic used as a stone replacement, and that cold and acidic conditions increase release. Source E2Source T7
  • Nanoplastics (under about 1 micrometre) are very hard to measure. Reviews call for better methods, and research groups are only now studying release from road wear and runoff. There is not yet enough data to give a number.
  • How our design reduces the risk. Plastic is fully melted and blended with a compatibiliser, so it is bonded into the binder rather than present as separate particles. The dose is small, about 6% of the binder, which is roughly 0.3% of the road by weight.
  • What we will measure. At the truck-yard pilot, we will collect runoff water and surface wear debris from Smart Asphalt and from the normal asphalt beside it, and analyse them for micro- and nanoplastics with a university laboratory. If the results are not acceptable, we change the design before any public road.

Will the capsules survive the hot mix?

Published research says capsules like ours can: seaweed-based capsules made at TU Delft survived asphalt production and compaction and stayed intact in X-ray scans. Our own smaller, reinforced capsules have not been tested yet, and proving this is the very first thing our lab work does.

The detailed answer
  • The challenge. Asphalt is mixed at about 150–170°C with sharp stones, then rolled flat under heavy compactors. A capsule has to survive all of that, yet still break when a crack reaches it.
  • What is published. Calcium-alginate capsules containing a rejuvenator were mixed into asphalt by TU Delft researchers; they survived production and compaction, and X-ray tomography showed them still round and intact. Source T1
  • How we design for survival. (1) A shell reinforced with nano-silica, which raises its strength and heat resistance. (2) Adding the capsules at the cooler end of mixing, with gentle stirring rather than high-speed blending. (3) Using warm-mix techniques to lower mixing temperatures by 20–40°C.
  • How we will prove it. Heat-resistance testing of the capsules (thermogravimetric analysis), then counting intact capsules in samples taken after mixing and compaction (microscopy and X-ray scans). The money for this is the largest part of our ask: 35%, KES 1.05 million.

Won’t the capsules just break under normal traffic?

That is the design intent to avoid, and it has to be tested. Capsules should stay shut under the even pressure of tyres and break where a crack concentrates stress. One published test saw many capsules break under repeated heavy loading, so we will tune shell strength in the lab and test under real trucks.

The detailed answer

Stress around the tip of a crack is far higher than the average pressure from a tyre, so a well-tuned shell should break mainly at cracks. But one study saw more than 80% of capsules rupture after 350 cycles of 0.6 MPa loading, which means capsules can also open under repeated heavy loads. Source T3 That is why our pilot is under heavy trucks: it is the hardest realistic test.

How much will it actually heal?

We do not know yet for our recipe. Published lab results range from only a few percent better than ordinary asphalt to around 80% recovery, depending on capsule design, temperature and test method. Measuring this is our second lab test.

The detailed answer

One porous-asphalt study at 23°C found capsules improved healing by only about 6 percentage points; others report up to about 80%. Warm temperatures help a lot. The numbers in our simulator and animation are illustrative design targets, not measurements. Source T2

Has Smart Asphalt been tested on a road?

Not yet. It is at the laboratory-validation stage (Technology Readiness Level 4–5): each part is backed by published research, and the combination is our design. The pilot is the next step.

The detailed answer

See the plan for the four stages: lab tests, the Dandora truck-yard pilot, the sorting hub, then a public road with KURA or KeNHA approval.

How is this different from plastic roads or the Japanese study in Nairobi?

Others make roads stronger. We make them heal. Plastic-modified roads exist (India, and a Japanese PET-asphalt study is running in Nairobi until 2027). Smart Asphalt adds self-healing capsules, uses the low-value plastic film nobody buys, and builds its supply chain with Dandora’s waste pickers.

The detailed answer

The Japanese consortium (Nippon Road, Kao and Shimizu, under JICA) uses PET bottle plastic, which already has buyers in Kenya. Source K2Source T4 Their presence is good news: it shows serious companies see a market for waste-based road materials in Kenya.

What happens at the end of the road’s life?

Like normal asphalt, it can be milled up and recycled into new asphalt. How the plastic behaves through repeated recycling is not yet well studied, so we will test recycled Smart Asphalt in the lab before scaling.

The detailed answer

Spent capsule shells are seaweed-based and biodegradable. The open question is the plastic fraction after several recycling cycles, which we list as a research item.

Do you need permission from road authorities?

Yes, for public roads. That is why we start on a private truck-parking yard in Dandora, where the owner has agreed in principle, and only then go to KURA or KeNHA with data. In the long run we want a Kenya Standard developed with KEBS.

The detailed answer

Starting on private land lets us gather real-world data quickly and safely, which is what regulators will ask for.

Will it cost more?

Slightly more to build, much less to keep. In our model the road costs about 3% more to build but about 30% less over 20 years, because it needs far fewer repairs. These are model numbers, to be proven in the pilot.

The detailed answer

The bitumen saved almost pays for our additive. The big saving comes from fewer repairs, which depends on the healing working. Source M3

Is there really enough waste, and is it free?

There is far more than we need, but it is not free: we pay waste pickers for it, on purpose. Some materials, like used cooking oil and bagasse, already have other buyers, so we focus on plastic film, which recyclers rarely want.

The detailed answer

Our five-year target would use roughly 180 tonnes of plastic a year, a tiny share of the 2,000+ tonnes reaching Dandora every day. The pilot road needs only about 70 litres of used cooking oil. Source D1Source W3
